## 1. Finding and evidence boundary

**Finding:** ARES-01 has a functioning mission-game prototype and several tested engineering subfeatures. Its intended design addresses the challenge's central purpose, but the implementation does **not yet demonstrate the complete, causally connected mission-design experience**. The isolated budget evaluator is newer than the baseline matrix and must be credited separately from its still-missing game integration.

Implementation states below are PARTIAL, MISSING, INCORRECT or VERIFIED SUBFEATURE; they are not NASA judgments or percentage completion. Static inspection alone does not establish tested functionality. Recommendations and educational copy are proposed unless explicitly described as incorporated in this document.

## 4. NASA-supported educational presentation incorporated here

### 4.1 Start with what the mission must return

DDS describes four interacting segments: launch gets hardware into space; space hardware makes observations; ground systems receive and manage them; users turn returned data into useful knowledge. Its design sequence runs from goals through requirements and verification, while ConOps describes how the mission operates over time. Requirements should have a way to check whether they were met. ([DDS](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/250323-design-and-develop-science-mission.pdf), pp. 8, 10, 13, 18–19.)

**INFERRED — ARES-01 application:** the player's D3 ranking should explain why an instrument is carried; D4/D5/D6/D7/D15 should explain whether that objective can be supported; the sol plan should explain when it is attempted; S8 should distinguish observations made from evidence returned. The four segments map to launch selection/checks, rover plus relay/payload, the communications/operations team, and the final scientific report. This is an educational mapping, not a new four-stage game mechanic.

### 4.2 A useful design is a coordinated design

MDL combines specialists, tools and collaborative studies. Its subsystem work records requirements, alternatives/trades, interfaces, mass/power/data estimates, risks and costs, considering a mission from launch through end-of-mission. ([MDL](https://etd.gsfc.nasa.gov/capabilities/integrated-design-center/mission-design-lab/), introduction, study types, capabilities and discipline inputs.)

**INFERRED — ARES-01 application:** a crew recommendation should connect its own specialty to the shared mission. Accepting a payload proposal should leave a readable reason showing the affected science access and margins. A communications improvement may consume budget otherwise available for instruments. This supports the existing six-seat deterministic design; it does not import MDL's tools, claim a NASA-reviewed design, or add multiplayer.

### 4.3 Concrete ARES-01 trade example

**VERIFIED — specified calculation, not NASA-priced hardware:** GAMEPLAY §4 defines all seven instruments at 300 USD million, fixed costs at 2,155, heavy launch at 245, and relay O1/O2/O3 at 0/80/140. Therefore the full payload with heavy launch costs **2,700 / 2,780 / 2,840**, respectively. Removing SuperCam and SHERLOC removes **105**: with O2 the total becomes **2,675**, leaving **25**. These are the specified game accounting values, with assumed instrument/vehicle/relay costs; the platform residual is calculated using assumptions. Do not portray these allocations as NASA's published instrument invoices.

**PROPOSED — future teaching at S3/S4:** explain what a chosen relay costs and what instrument removal gives up, using the evaluator's actual line items and the approved objective-access table. A financially feasible design is not automatically launchable: vehicle capacity, landing and science feasibility remain independent checks. The missing science/input tables prevent asserting a particular completed objective from this example. The 20-sol over-budget consequence is specified but not yet integrated.
